\begin{abstract}
Sparse-reward navigation tasks challenge conventional reinforcement learning because
the agent receives informative feedback only at terminal states, making per-step
credit assignment difficult.
Direct policy search via genetic algorithms (GAs) bypasses this difficulty by encoding
a complete state-to-action mapping as an evolvable genotype and evaluating it through
episode-level fitness.
This paper presents an empirical study of GA-based direct policy search on the
deterministic $8\times8$ FrozenLake-v1 benchmark.
Each individual encodes a tabular policy of 64 discrete actions; a shaped fitness
function combines a goal-success reward, a Manhattan-distance potential, a per-step
cost, and a hole-fall penalty to provide informative gradients across the sparse
landscape.
Three research questions are investigated: which combination of crossover operator,
mutation operator, and replacement strategy achieves the best performance; how
sensitive GA performance is to operator rates, population size, and tournament size;
and how fitness weight choices alter evolved behavior.
Results show that five of thirty tested operator combinations consistently achieve
the theoretical maximum fitness of 108.60 with 100\% success rate and zero
run-to-run variance.
Rate sensitivity analysis reveals that a mutation rate of 0.01 is critically
dependent on crossover rate, while a rate of 0.2 achieves peak fitness regardless
of crossover configuration.
An aggressive step penalty of 2.0 per step collapses performance to 0\% success,
demonstrating that shaping balance is as critical as operator design.
A scalability study reveals qualitatively distinct convergence regimes: the
$8\times8$ grid reaches a high-fitness plateau by generation~20, the $16\times16$
grid converges much later via step-like jumps and reaches a comparable plateau only
after generation~100, while the $32\times32$ grid fails to improve meaningfully
within the same generation budget.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a principled framework for sequential
decision-making, but sparse reward settings---where the reward signal is issued only
at terminal states---introduce a severe credit-assignment problem.
Value-function-based methods struggle to propagate meaningful learning signals
backward through long trajectories when feedback is so infrequent~\cite{b1}.
Evolutionary computation (EC) offers a complementary approach: by encoding a complete
policy as a chromosome and evaluating it through full-episode fitness, the selection
signal naturally reflects trajectory-level performance without requiring per-step
gradient information.

FrozenLake-v1 is a canonical gridworld benchmark in which an agent must navigate an
$N\times N$ frozen surface from a start cell to a goal cell while avoiding holes.
In the deterministic (non-slippery) variant, the transition function is fully specified,
making it a tractable but non-trivial benchmark for policy search: the policy space
is large ($4^{N^2}$ possible deterministic policies), rewards are sparse (0 at every
step, 1 only upon reaching the goal), and outcomes are binary.
Its simplicity makes it useful for controlled empirical study, while its sparsity
motivates the use of fitness shaping.

This paper applies GA direct policy search to the deterministic $8\times8$
FrozenLake-v1 environment.
Each individual encodes a tabular policy as a 64-gene integer chromosome, with genes
representing discrete actions \{Left, Down, Right, Up\} for each state.
A multi-component fitness function provides gradient information beyond the sparse
binary reward.
The GA operates over 100 generations with a population of 100 individuals.

The objectives of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
\item Identify which combination of crossover method, mutation method, and
      replacement strategy produces the highest and most consistent fitness
      on the $8\times8$ map.
\item Quantify the sensitivity of GA performance to mutation rate, crossover rate,
      population size, and tournament size.
\item Determine how fitness weight configuration affects evolved behavior,
      comparing standard shaping against heavy hole and step penalties.
\item Characterize how GA convergence changes as grid size increases from
      $8\times8$ to $16\times16$ and $32\times32$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contributions of this work}:
\begin{itemize}
\item An exhaustive comparison of all 30 operator-strategy combinations
      ($3$ crossovers $\times$ $5$ mutations $\times$ $2$ replacement strategies)
      with statistical testing on a fixed deterministic $8\times8$ FrozenLake-v1 map.
\item Heatmap-based sensitivity analysis over mutation/crossover rates and
      population/tournament sizes.
\item An empirical demonstration that an overly aggressive step penalty
      (2.0 per step) completely destabilizes GA convergence, identifying a
      critical fitness-shaping failure mode.
\item A scalability study across $8\times8$, $16\times16$, and $32\times32$ grids
      revealing qualitatively distinct convergence regimes, including complete
      convergence failure for $32\times32$ under a fixed generation budget.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~II reviews related work.
Section~III describes the methodology.
Section~IV presents experimental results.
Section~V concludes with a discussion of findings and future directions.

\section{Literature Review}

Genetic algorithms have been established as a principled approach to reinforcement
learning policy search.
Whiteson~\cite{b1} characterizes direct policy search---encoding a complete
state-to-action mapping as an evolvable individual---as particularly suited to
sparse-reward, discrete-action environments, because episode-level fitness naturally
handles the credit-assignment problem that challenges value-function methods.
Tournament selection is identified as providing tunable selection pressure through a
single parameter, directly motivating our configurable tournament size.
Faycal and Zito~\cite{b2} apply this framework directly to FrozenLake-v1, evolving
neural-network weights and demonstrating that two operator modifications
(multi-step mutation and directed crossover) reduce convergence from over 500 to
under 150 generations (Mann-Whitney~U, $p < 0.01$).
Though their representation is neural-network weights whereas ours is a tabular direct
encoding, the core result---that crossover and mutation operator choice substantially
alters convergence speed---directly motivates Experiment~1.

The systematic analysis of operator combinations and parameter interactions is the
focus of two complementary works.
Song et al.~\cite{b3} survey reinforcement learning-assisted evolutionary algorithms
(RL-EA) across five integration categories and identify adaptive operator selection as
consistently outperforming fixed-operator configurations in convergence speed and
solution quality.
Ma et al.~\cite{b4} provide a concrete instantiation: a deep reinforcement learning
agent (double DQN) selects both operator type and rates at each generation for
flexible job shop scheduling, outperforming fixed-rate baselines.
Their finding that adapting both operator type and rate is necessary---neither alone
suffices---motivates our systematic separation of operator-type comparison
(Experiment~1) from rate sensitivity (Experiments~2a and~2b).

Fitness function design is a critical dimension of GA-based navigation.
Zhu et al.~\cite{b5} demonstrate that potential-based reward shaping, integrated with
a genetic local search framework, can increase cumulative reward by up to 39.6\% in
sparse-reward gridworld tasks while reducing training variance.
Their theoretical result---that potential-based shaping is policy-preserving---provides
formal justification for our Manhattan-distance term, which assigns non-zero fitness to
every episode and acts as a surrogate potential over the goal-distance landscape.
Experiment~2c directly tests the sensitivity of this shaping design.

The theoretical framework for interpreting our empirical sensitivities is provided by
fitness landscape theory.
Srivastava et al.~\cite{b6} formalise navigability as the probability of reaching the
global fitness peak via mutation and selection, showing that sign epistasis---where a
mutation's fitness effect reverses depending on genetic background---demands a careful
exploration--exploitation balance.
The FrozenLake policy space ($4^{64} \approx 3.4\times10^{38}$ possible policies) is
highly epistatic: changing the action at a single state adjacent to a hole can flip
fitness from maximum to minimum.
This landscape structure informs the expected sensitivities in Experiments~2a, 2b,
and~3, where the fitness landscape becomes progressively more rugged as grid size grows.
Together, these six studies establish the theoretical and empirical foundations of our
investigation, spanning GA direct policy search~\cite{b1,b2}, operator and parameter
analysis~\cite{b3,b4}, fitness shaping~\cite{b5}, and landscape theory~\cite{b6}.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Environment Setup}

The environment is FrozenLake-v1 from Gymnasium, configured with
\texttt{is\_slippery=False} for fully deterministic transitions.
The default grid size is $8\times8$ (64 states).
A single random map is generated via \texttt{generate\_random\_map(size=8)} before any
experiment begins and reused identically across all experimental conditions, ensuring a
fair comparison across operator variants and parameter settings.
The goal is at state index $(7,7)$ (bottom-right corner).
The maximum episode length is capped at
$\textit{max\_steps} = \textit{size}^{2} \times 2 = 128$ steps.
Evaluation is single-episode per individual; because the environment is deterministic,
single-episode fitness is fully reproducible for a fixed genotype and map.

\subsection{Individual Representation}

Each individual encodes a complete deterministic tabular policy as a fixed-length
integer chromosome of length 64 (one gene per state).
Each gene takes a value in $\{0,1,2,3\}$, corresponding to actions
\{Left, Down, Right, Up\} respectively.
Initialization is uniform random over $\{0,1,2,3\}$ for all genes.
This direct encoding avoids function approximation and supports a clean study of
operator effects on a discrete policy space.

\subsection{Fitness Function}

The fitness of an individual is computed from a single episode as:
\begin{equation}
F = s \cdot w_s + \frac{w_m}{1 + d_{\text{Manhattan}}} - t \cdot w_{\text{step}} - h \cdot w_h
\label{eq:fitness}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $s \in \{0,1\}$ indicates goal-reaching success;
$d_{\text{Manhattan}}$ is the Manhattan distance from the agent's terminal state to
the goal; $t$ is the number of steps taken; $h \in \{0,1\}$ indicates whether the
agent fell into a hole.
Default weights from \texttt{config.json}: $w_s = 100.0$, $w_m = 10.0$,
$w_{\text{step}} = 0.1$, $w_h = 50.0$.

The Manhattan-distance term in \eqref{eq:fitness} provides a non-zero gradient signal
across all episodes regardless of goal-reaching success, acting as a surrogate
potential function analogous to the potential-based shaping mechanisms
of~\cite{b5}.
Under default weights with optimal navigation ($s=1$, $d=0$, $t=14$), the theoretical
maximum fitness is $F_{\max} = 100 + 10 - 1.4 = 108.60$.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithm Pipeline}

The GA loop proceeds as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Initialization}: Generate $\mu = 100$ individuals with uniformly random genotypes.
\item \textit{Evaluation}: Single-episode fitness evaluation for each individual.
\item \textit{Selection}: Tournament selection (default tournament size $k=3$),
      sampling without replacement and returning the individual with highest fitness.
\item \textit{Crossover}: Applied with probability $p_c = 0.8$.
      Three methods are implemented:
      one-point (single cut-point), two-point (two cut-points exchanging a middle
      segment), and uniform (each gene independently drawn from either parent with
      equal probability).
      If crossover is not triggered, both parents are cloned.
\item \textit{Mutation}: Applied to each offspring after crossover.
      Two operator categories exist:
      \textit{per-gene operators} (random resetting, swap) mutate each gene
      independently with probability $p_m = 0.1$;
      \textit{whole-individual operators} (insert, scramble, inversion) fire once per
      individual with probability $p_m$.
      Note that $p_m$ has different semantics for these two categories, making direct
      rate comparisons between them not straightforward.
\item \textit{Replacement}: Two strategies are compared.
      $(\mu{+}\lambda)$: merge parents and offspring, retain the top $\mu$ by fitness.
      $(\mu,\lambda)$: discard parents, retain the top $\mu$ from offspring only.
\item \textit{Termination}: After 100 generations.
\end{enumerate}

Metrics logged per generation: best fitness, mean fitness, population success rate.
Metrics reported per run: best individual's fitness, success flag, and steps taken.

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}

All experiments use $n = 5$ independent runs per condition with the same globally
fixed $8\times8$ random map.
Results are reported as means across runs.
Experiments~2a--2c each vary a single axis of the default configuration while holding
all other parameters at their defaults.
Experiment~3 independently generates new random maps for the $16\times16$ and
$32\times32$ grid sizes.
Default configuration: $\mu = 100$, $T_{\max} = 100$, $p_c = 0.8$, $p_m = 0.1$,
$k = 3$, one-point crossover, random-resetting mutation, $(\mu{+}\lambda)$ strategy.

\section{Experimental Work}

\subsection{Experiment 1: Operator Combination Comparison}

\textit{Goal}: Identify which combination of crossover operator, mutation operator,
and replacement strategy achieves the highest and most consistent fitness.

\textit{Setup}: All 30 combinations ($3$ crossovers $\times$ $5$ mutations $\times$
$2$ strategies) are tested for $n=5$ independent runs each on the same fixed
$8\times8$ map with default rates ($p_c=0.8$, $p_m=0.1$, $\mu=100$, $k=3$).
Source: \texttt{results/exp1\_combinations.csv}.

\textit{Findings}:
Five of thirty combinations achieve mean fitness 108.60 with standard deviation 0.00
across all five runs: $(\mu{+}\lambda)$ + uniform + random resetting;
$(\mu{+}\lambda)$ + uniform + swap;
$(\mu,\lambda)$ + one-point + random resetting;
$(\mu,\lambda)$ + two-point + random resetting; and
$(\mu,\lambda)$ + two-point + inversion.
All five achieve 100\% success rate and exactly 14 mean steps, matching the
theoretical fitness maximum.
The zero standard deviation reflects that the GA converged to the same optimal
policy on every run---a consequence of the fully deterministic environment.
The $t$-test between the top two configurations produced $p = \text{NaN}$, because the
Welch $t$-test is undefined when both groups have zero variance.
This is not a test failure; it reflects complete convergence: any run of these
configurations on this deterministic task finds an optimal 14-step policy every time.
The worst result was $(\mu{+}\lambda)$ + one-point + inversion
(mean $= -7.81$, success rate $= 0\%$).

\textit{Interpretation}:
Uniform crossover preserves diversity by independently recombining each gene, which is
well-suited to a tabular representation where gene position carries no intrinsic meaning.
Random resetting directly explores the discrete action space by replacing a gene with
a uniformly sampled value, making it the most natural mutation operator for this
encoding.
In contrast, insert, scramble, and inversion are permutation-style operators that
rearrange gene order: they preserve the multiset of actions in the genotype but
reassign which action maps to which state.
For tabular direct encoding, rearranging assignments is generally harmful because each
gene's fitness contribution depends on its state-index semantics, not its position
relative to neighboring genes~\cite{b2}.

\textit{Caveats}: $n=5$ runs is modest; zero-variance results reflect the deterministic
task structure rather than universal robustness across map topologies.

\subsection{Experiment 2a: Mutation Rate and Crossover Rate Sensitivity}

\textit{Goal}: Map how fitness varies jointly with mutation rate and crossover rate.

\textit{Setup}: Grid search over $p_m \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.2\}$ and
$p_c \in \{0.5, 0.7, 0.9\}$ (9 conditions, $n=5$ runs each).
Default operator: one-point crossover, random-resetting mutation,
$(\mu{+}\lambda)$ strategy.
Source: \texttt{results/exp2a\_heatmap\_data.csv}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{results/exp2a_heatmap.png}}
\caption{Average fitness as a function of mutation rate (rows) and crossover
rate (columns). Low mutation ($p_m=0.01$) with low crossover ($p_c=0.5$) yields
catastrophic failure ($-8.88$); mutation rate $\geq 0.2$ achieves peak fitness
across all crossover rates.}
\label{fig:exp2a}
\end{figure}

The heatmap in Fig.~\ref{fig:exp2a} reveals a clear pattern.
At $p_m=0.01$, $p_c=0.5$, the GA fails entirely (mean $= -8.88$, 0\% success):
minimal mutation combined with infrequent crossover means that roughly half of offspring
per generation are exact clones of their parents, with the other half receiving only
marginal perturbations.
Population diversity collapses quickly, trapping the search in a low-fitness local
optimum.
Increasing $p_c$ to 0.7--0.9 at the same $p_m=0.01$ recovers partial performance
(mean $\approx 61.6$), indicating that crossover can partially compensate for low
mutation.
At $p_m=0.2$, peak fitness (108.60) is achieved across all three crossover rates,
suggesting that sufficient per-gene diversity maintenance from mutation makes
crossover rate secondary.

\textit{Interpretation}: This pattern is consistent with fitness landscape theory~\cite{b6}:
the FrozenLake policy space contains sign epistasis where single-gene changes at
critical states can drastically alter fitness.
High mutation rates maintain the diversity required to escape such local optima;
crossover rate matters most when mutation rate is insufficient on its own.

\textit{Caveats}: Coarse three-level grid; results are specific to one-point crossover
and random-resetting mutation.

\subsection{Experiment 2b: Population Size and Tournament Size Sensitivity}

\textit{Goal}: Quantify the joint effect of population size and tournament size
(selection pressure) on GA performance.

\textit{Setup}: Grid search over $\mu \in \{20, 50, 200\}$ and
$k \in \{2, 5, 10\}$ (conditions with $k > \mu$ are omitted), $n=5$ runs each.
Source: \texttt{results/exp2b\_sensitivity\_data.csv}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{results/exp2b_sensitivity.png}}
\caption{Average fitness as a function of population size (rows) and tournament
size (columns). Tournament size $k=5$ is optimal for small populations; excessive
selection pressure ($k=10$) causes premature convergence at small population sizes.}
\label{fig:exp2b}
\end{figure}

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:exp2b}, tournament size $k=5$ with $\mu \in \{20, 50\}$
achieves peak fitness (108.60).
Tournament size $k=10$ with these small populations yields mean fitness $\approx 38$,
reflecting excessive selection pressure: a tournament of 10 from a population of 20
amounts to selecting from the top half at each step, rapidly concentrating the
population around a few high-fitness individuals and eliminating diversity needed to
escape local optima.
Population $\mu=200$ is tolerant of both $k=2$ and $k=10$ (both achieve 108.60),
likely because 200 individuals provide sufficient diversity to buffer against extreme
selection pressures.
The anomalous result at $\mu=200$, $k=5$ (85.17 instead of 108.60) likely reflects
sampling variance given $n=5$.

\textit{Interpretation}: Selection pressure must be matched to population size.
The navigability analysis in~\cite{b6} predicts that loss of diversity through
excessive selection is particularly harmful in rugged, epistatic landscapes---exactly
the profile of the FrozenLake policy space.

\textit{Caveats}: Only 5 runs per condition; the $\mu=200$, $k=5$ anomaly warrants
further investigation.

\subsection{Experiment 2c: Fitness Weight Sensitivity}

\textit{Goal}: Determine how the choice of fitness weight configuration alters evolved
behavior and final performance.

\textit{Setup}: Three weight configurations tested, $n=5$ runs each.
\textit{Standard}: $w_s=100$, $w_m=10$, $w_{\text{step}}=0.1$, $w_h=50$.
\textit{Heavy\_Hole}: $w_h$ increased to 200.0.
\textit{Heavy\_Step}: $w_{\text{step}}$ increased to 2.0.
Source: \texttt{results/exp2c\_weights.csv}.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{results/exp2c_weights_analysis.png}}

The Heavy Step Penalty collapses performance to 0\% success and 1 mean step;

\label{fig:exp2c}

Table~\ref{tab:exp2c} and Fig.~\ref{fig:exp2c} show the results.
The standard configuration achieves perfect fitness (108.60), 100\% success, and
14 mean steps---the minimum path length from $(0,0)$ to $(7,7)$.
The Heavy\_Hole configuration ($w_h=200$) reduces success to 80\% with 31.2 mean
steps, suggesting the GA evolves overly cautious policies that take longer, circuitous
routes to avoid any proximity to holes, at the cost of overall success reliability.

The Heavy\_Step configuration ($w_{\text{step}}=2.0$) is a catastrophic failure:
0\% success, 1.0 mean steps, and mean fitness $-51.29$.
A step penalty of 2.0 per step would incur $-256$ over 128 steps, completely
overwhelming the positive signals.
The GA learns to minimise steps by immediately terminating---the easiest path is to
fall into the first adjacent hole in a single step.
The observed mean fitness of $-51.29$ is consistent with the equation in
\eqref{eq:fitness}: a 1-step terminal transition into a hole at state $(1,0)$
(Manhattan distance 13 from the goal) gives
$0 + 10/14 - 2.0 - 50.0 \approx -51.3$.

\textit{Interpretation}: This is a concrete illustration of the fitness shaping
pitfall noted by~\cite{b5}: a penalty that dominates the fitness function causes the
GA to optimise for that component alone at the expense of the true objective.
The standard balanced configuration is essential for reliable convergence.

\textit{Caveats}: Only three weight configurations tested; intermediate values of
$w_{\text{step}}$ between 0.1 and 2.0 were not explored.

\subsection{Experiment 3: Scalability Across Grid Sizes}

\textit{Goal}: Characterize how GA convergence rate and final performance change
as grid size (and thus policy-space size) grows.

\textit{Setup}: Three grid sizes---$8\times8$ (genotype length 64), $16\times16$
(length 256), $32\times32$ (length 1024)---tested with $\mu=150$,
$T_{\max}=150$, $n=5$ runs each.
The $8\times8$ map uses the same global map as previous experiments; $16\times16$ and
$32\times32$ use independently generated random maps.
Source: \texttt{results/exp3\_scalability\_ci.png}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{results/exp3_scalability_ci.png}}
\caption{Scalability study: mean best fitness with shaded variability band across
150 generations for grid sizes $8\times8$, $16\times16$, and $32\times32$
($\mu=150$, $n=5$ runs).
The $8\times8$ curve reaches a high-fitness plateau by approximately
generation~20; the $16\times16$ curve improves later via step-like jumps,
reaching a comparable plateau only after generation~100; the $32\times32$ curve
remains near its initial fitness level throughout, indicating failure to discover
higher-quality policies under the same budget.}
\label{fig:exp3}
\end{figure}

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:exp3}, the $8\times8$ and $16\times16$ conditions both
achieve high final fitness, but their convergence dynamics differ substantially.
The $8\times8$ curve stays near its initial level for the first $\approx$10
generations, then rises sharply and reaches a plateau around $\approx$108 by
approximately generation~20, remaining essentially flat thereafter.
In contrast, the $16\times16$ curve stays near baseline for much longer, beginning
to improve only around generation~50 and rising in a series of step-like
jumps---roughly in the generation ranges 50--70, 80--90, and 100--105---before
reaching a comparable plateau near $\approx$108 shortly after generation~100.

The $32\times32$ curve, however, remains almost flat around its initial fitness level
(approximately $-10$) across all 150 generations, with no visible sustained
improvement.
This indicates that for the $32\times32$ random map instance used here, the fixed
search budget (150 individuals $\times$ 150 generations) and the default
operator/parameter configuration were insufficient to reliably discover
higher-quality policies.

\textit{Interpretation}: With increasing genotype length ($64 \to 256 \to 1024$),
the effective search difficulty increases sharply.
This manifests in the plot as (i)~much later convergence for $16\times16$ relative
to $8\times8$, and (ii)~a complete lack of progress for $32\times32$ under the same
budget.
This qualitative pattern is consistent with the fitness landscape perspective
of~\cite{b6}: as dimensionality grows, beneficial mutation pathways become rarer and
the landscape harder to navigate, making convergence increasingly dependent on larger
evaluation budgets, better-tuned operator rates, and/or adaptive operator/parameter
control~\cite{b3,b4}.

\textit{Caveats}: The $16\times16$ and $32\times32$ conditions use independently
generated random maps, so grid-size effects are confounded with map-instance
difficulty; the flat $32\times32$ curve may reflect an unusually difficult map
instance rather than grid size alone.
Additionally, the shaded regions reflect variability across only 5 runs and should
be interpreted as indicative rather than as definitive confidence intervals.
A stronger scalability conclusion would require multiple random map instances per
size and a generation budget scaled with genotype length.

\section{Conclusion and Discussion}

\textbf{Key Empirical Findings}:
\begin{itemize}
\item Five of thirty tested operator combinations achieve the theoretical maximum
      fitness (108.60, 100\% success, 14 mean steps) with zero variance across five
      independent runs.
      Per-gene mutation operators (random resetting, swap) dominate successful
      configurations; whole-individual positional operators (insert, scramble,
      inversion) are generally poorly suited to the tabular direct encoding.
\item Operator semantics matter independently of rate: insert, scramble, and inversion
      rearrange gene positions rather than replacing gene values, making them
      structurally mismatched to a representation where gene identity---not position---
      carries all policy information.
\item Mutation rate is the primary diversity-maintenance lever.
      A rate of 0.2 achieves peak fitness across all tested crossover rates; at 0.01,
      fitness is critically sensitive to crossover rate and can collapse to $-8.88$.
\item Tournament size must be matched to population size.
      Tournament size 10 with populations of 20--50 causes premature convergence
      (mean fitness $\approx 38$); tournament size 5 is optimal for these smaller
      populations.
\item Fitness shaping balance is as critical as operator design.
      An aggressive step penalty (2.0 per step) entirely destroys convergence,
      reducing success to 0\% and evolving policies that immediately self-terminate.
\item The scalability study reveals qualitatively distinct convergence regimes:
      $8\times8$ converges rapidly by generation~20, $16\times16$ converges via
      step-like jumps reaching a comparable high-fitness plateau only after
      generation~100, and $32\times32$ shows no meaningful improvement within the
      same 150-generation budget, demonstrating that search difficulty grows sharply
      with genotype length.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations}:
\begin{itemize}
\item $n=5$ runs per condition is modest; higher-variance configurations may require
      20--30 runs for reliable statistical conclusions.
\item All experiments share one fixed random $8\times8$ map; results may not
      generalise to other map topologies.
\item The mutation rate parameter has different semantic interpretations for per-gene
      and whole-individual operators, confounding direct comparisons between these
      operator classes.
\item Experiment~3 uses different maps for $16\times16$ and $32\times32$, so
      grid-size effects and map-instance difficulty are confounded; the flat
      $32\times32$ curve may reflect an unusually difficult map instance rather
      than grid size alone. Additionally, the shaded variability bands reflect
      only 5 runs and are indicative rather than definitive confidence intervals.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Future Work}:
\begin{itemize}
\item Replace fixed-rate operator selection with an adaptive RL-based scheme
      trained on the empirical baselines established in Experiments~1--2, following
      the RL-EA framework of~\cite{b3,b4}.
\item Extend Experiment~3 to test multiple random map instances per grid size,
      separating topology effects from scaling effects.
\item Normalise mutation magnitude across operator types (e.g., fix the expected
      number of changed genes) to enable fair comparisons between per-gene and
      whole-individual operators.
\item Investigate multi-episode evaluation with a stochastic map
      (\texttt{is\_slippery=True}) to test policy robustness under uncertainty.
\item Explore intermediate fitness weight values (especially $w_{\text{step}}$
      between 0.1 and 2.0) to identify the threshold at which the step penalty
      becomes counterproductive.
\end{itemize}
